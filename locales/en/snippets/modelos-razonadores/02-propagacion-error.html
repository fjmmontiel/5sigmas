Error propagation through reasoning chains
An error in step 2 of 6 does not stay there: later steps amplify it because they build on an incorrect premise.

Problem: "How many days are there from March 15 to June 10, counting both endpoints?" The error introduced at step 2 contaminates every later result.

1 · CORRECT

"I need to count the days from March 15 through June 10, including both endpoints."

Correct understanding of the task.
↓
2 · ERROR HERE

"March has 31 days. From the 15th to the 31st: 31 − 15 = 16 days."

Off by one: including March 15 gives 17 days.
↓
3 · CONTAMINATED

"April contributes 30 full days."

Correct in isolation, but now built on the wrong running total.
↓
4 · CONTAMINATED

"May contributes 31 full days."

Again correct in isolation.
↓
5 · CONTAMINATED

"June 1 through June 10 contributes 10 days."

Correct in isolation.
↓
6 · WRONG RESULT

"Total: 16 + 30 + 31 + 10 = 87 days."

The correct result is 88 days. The chain looks coherent even though the initial error survived intact.

Length matters

Longer chains create more opportunities for an error to enter and propagate. More steps do not automatically mean more reliability.

The final answer is not enough

A model can reach the right conclusion through wrong steps, or the wrong conclusion after several apparently valid steps.

Intermediate verification

Checkpoints contain propagation. If step 2 is verified before step 3 begins, the error can be stopped instead of carried to the end.
