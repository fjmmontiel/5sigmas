# Chapter 3 — Measurement: GDP vs well-being

When we ask whether an economy is “better” than before, or whether a technology is improving people’s lives, we need a measurement model. GDP is often used for that purpose because it is standardized, frequent and comparable. But GDP is a measure of **market production**, not a complete measure of well-being.

This chapter separates those concepts and shows how to combine economic activity with health, education, distribution and subjective outcomes when evaluating technological change.

## 1. What GDP measures

GDP sums the market value of final goods and services produced in an economy over a period. It is valuable because it provides a coherent measure of economic activity that can be compared across time and countries.

The limitation is not that GDP measures the wrong thing. The limitation is treating what it measures as if it were the whole outcome.

{{ include_html("snippets/ia-pib-bienestar-energia/03-pib-bienestar.html") }}

Several important dimensions sit partly or completely outside GDP:

- **Unpaid work:** care, household production and volunteering often do not pass through a market transaction.
- **Distribution:** GDP per capita is an average and does not say how gains are shared.
- **Quality:** more spending or more units of a service do not automatically mean better outcomes.
- **Non-market conditions:** safety, trust, environmental quality, leisure and social support need separate indicators.

---

## 2. Material and non-material outcomes

A useful well-being framework keeps both categories visible.

**Material outcomes** include income, employment, housing, health services and education. GDP is informative here because economic capacity affects all of them, but distribution and service quality still matter.

**Non-material outcomes** include personal security, social relationships, work-life balance, civic participation, environmental quality and how people evaluate their own lives.

{{ include_html("snippets/ia-pib-energia/series_energy_ai_03_measurement.html") }}

The point is not to replace economic measurement with subjective surveys. It is to avoid asking one indicator to answer several different questions.

## 3. Income and subjective well-being

People with higher income tend, on average, to report higher life evaluation. But the relationship is heterogeneous rather than one universal curve.

The long-running Easterlin debate asks why higher-income people within a country tend to report greater well-being while long-run national income growth does not always produce a proportional rise in average life satisfaction.

A 2023 adversarial collaboration by Kahneman, Killingsworth and Mellers helped reconcile apparently conflicting results: for most participants, reported well-being continued to increase with income, while a lower-well-being subgroup showed a flatter relationship at higher income levels.

{{ include_html("snippets/ia-pib-bienestar-energia/03-kahneman-killingsworth.html") }}

The measurement lesson matters more than any single threshold: **population averages can hide different response curves across subgroups**.

---

## 4. Complementary frameworks

Several widely used frameworks broaden the outcome space beyond production alone:

| Framework | Adds to the picture |
| --- | --- |
| Human Development Index | health, education and income |
| Inequality-adjusted HDI | distribution of those capabilities |
| OECD Better Life | housing, jobs, community, environment, safety and life satisfaction |
| Subjective well-being surveys | how people evaluate and experience their own lives |

No one framework is a universal replacement for GDP. Each embeds a different answer to the question “what counts as progress?”

That is why the best measurement stack is plural: use GDP for production, distributional measures for who receives gains, sector metrics for quality and access, and well-being indicators when lived outcomes are part of the question.

---

## 5. What this changes when evaluating AI

AI can create value that appears in several places at different times:

1. **Task output:** a person or team completes a task faster or at higher quality.
2. **Firm productivity:** workflows are redesigned and the task-level gain survives at organization scale.
3. **Market output:** prices, quantities and new products change enough to appear in economic statistics.
4. **Distribution:** gains and costs are spread across workers, firms and consumers in different ways.
5. **Well-being:** time, autonomy, access, security and quality of services may improve or worsen independently of headline output.

A technology can therefore raise measured productivity without improving every well-being dimension, or improve consumer experience before the gain is visible in GDP.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is GDP a bad metric?**  
No. It is a strong metric for market production. Problems arise when it is used as a complete proxy for human outcomes.

**Why is GDP per capita insufficient for distribution?**  
Because it is an average. Two economies can have the same GDP per person with very different distributions of income and access.

**Why include subjective well-being?**  
Because material conditions and how people experience their lives are related but not identical. Survey measures add information that production statistics cannot contain.

**How should AI impact be measured?**  
At several layers: task performance, firm productivity, market output, distribution and lived outcomes. No single number captures all of them.
