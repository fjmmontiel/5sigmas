# Chapter 1 — Electricity and well-being: the real mechanisms

This chapter describes the mechanisms through which access to electricity produces real effects in health, the economy and industry, and distinguishes between the amount of energy available and the quality of supply. By the end, the reader will understand the four main channels connecting electrification with well-being, know what the World Bank's Multi-Tier Framework (MTF) measures and why a nominal grid connection is not equivalent to effective energy access, and have the basis for understanding why AI infrastructure has the same physical dependencies as any other electricity-intensive sector.

The correlation between electricity and human development appears across the data with a regularity that is difficult to ignore. Countries with greater access to reliable, affordable electricity tend to have longer life expectancy, lower infant mortality, higher industrial productivity and a greater capacity to provide services.

The latest update from the International Energy Agency (IEA) shows why this relationship is becoming central again with AI: electricity consumption by data centers rose 17% in 2025, while consumption by AI-oriented centers grew even faster. Although energy use per task is falling because of efficiency improvements, total use continues to rise as adoption and intensive workloads, including agents, increase. Greater efficiency per query does not remove the need for a reliable grid to sustain the system as a whole ([IEA, 2026](https://www.iea.org/news/data-centre-electricity-use-surged-in-2025-even-with-tightening-bottlenecks-driving-a-scramble-for-solutions)).

The data also allow that regularity to be quantified. Panel-data analyses covering 47 countries estimate that a 1% increase in per-capita energy consumption is associated with an increase of between 0.64 and 0.94 percentage points in perceived well-being indices, a statistically significant relationship even after controlling for income, urbanization and climate conditions [Lee et al. (2020)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.34.1.122).

The problem is that correlation does not explain the mechanism. If we do not understand why electricity produces those effects, we also cannot anticipate when it stops producing them, what level of access is sufficient, or what form of supply actually matters.

This chapter breaks down the real mechanisms connecting electricity with well-being and distinguishes between two variables that are often conflated: the amount of energy available and the quality of supply.

## 1. The four main channels

Electricity does not produce well-being directly. It does so by enabling other systems that have a direct impact on people's lives.

{{ include_html("snippets/ia-pib-bienestar-energia/01-mecanismos.html") }}

### Health

The most immediate impact occurs in healthcare. Reliable refrigeration makes it possible to preserve vaccines, blood, medicines and laboratory samples. Diagnostic equipment, from a simple blood test to an MRI scanner, requires stable power. Adequate lighting in operating rooms and delivery rooms directly reduces perinatal mortality.

Outside healthcare facilities, the impact extends into the home: food refrigeration reduces the microbial load in the diet, safe drinking water requires pumping and treatment, and replacing indoor combustion from firewood or kerosene with electricity reduces chronic respiratory disease, one of the leading causes of mortality in low-income households. Case studies in Latin America document reductions of more than thirty percentage points in respiratory-disease indicators after this transition, with stronger effects among children under five and older adults [Lee et al. (2020)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.34.1.122).

### Logistics and industry

Electricity makes it possible to mechanize processes that would otherwise require intensive physical labor or simply would not be performed. From grain mills to irrigation systems, from cold chains for perishable food to basic manufacturing workshops, electrification multiplies the amount of work a person can perform in an hour.

In agriculture, access to electric irrigation pumps can double or triple yield per hectare in areas with seasonal water deficits. In manufacturing, the ability to operate electric machinery without relying on combustion engines with costly maintenance changes the economics of small and medium-sized firms.

### Services and education

Electricity brings night-time lighting, which extends useful hours for study and work; digital connectivity, which requires energy to operate; and telecommunications services, which need energized infrastructure. The chain is direct: electrification enables connectivity, connectivity enables access to information and markets, and that access translates into greater capacity for learning, entrepreneurship and economic participation.

## 2. The difference between quantity and quality

This is where the distinction hidden by aggregate data appears: having access to electricity is not the same as having access to high-quality electricity.

### Quantity: kilowatt-hours per capita

The most common metric for measuring electrification is electricity consumption in kWh per capita. It is useful for large-scale comparisons, but it has a fundamental limitation: it says nothing about how that consumption is distributed, at what price it is delivered, or how reliably the supply operates.

A country can have a relatively high average kWh per capita while entire regions experience twelve-hour daily outages and the capital consumes electricity continuously. The average hides a distribution with radically different consequences for each part of that distribution.

### Quality: reliability, stability and the cost of outages

Supply quality is measured with different variables:

- **Reliability:** hours of outage per year and their predictability. A system with four hours of predictable outage each day allows users to adapt through backup generators or process scheduling. A system with random outages of the same total duration causes greater damage because it prevents planning.

- **Voltage stability:** voltage fluctuations damage electronic equipment and appliances. For small industry, this means higher maintenance costs and shorter machinery lifetimes. For households, it means appliances failing more often than disposable income can replace them.

- **Cost of outages:** a supply interruption is not merely an inconvenience. For a clinic, an outage can mean losing the entire cold chain. For a workshop, it means halted production and missed orders. For a shop, it means failed refrigeration and spoiled goods. The real economic cost of outages is far greater than the visible cost of a kilowatt-hour.

### Thresholds and diminishing returns

The data show a nonlinear relationship between electricity consumption and well-being. At very low levels of access, each additional kilowatt-hour has an enormous impact: it can be the difference between having or not having lighting, basic refrigeration or connectivity. Beyond a certain threshold, the marginal impact declines.

> The first step—from zero access to reliable basic access—produces the largest returns in health and productivity. The World Bank's Multi-Tier Framework for Energy Access (MTF) identifies the range of 50 to 200 kWh per capita per year as the zone where returns in health and basic productivity are most pronounced, before demand shifts toward higher-consumption industrial and leisure uses [ESMAP, World Bank (2015)](https://documents.worldbank.org/en/publication/documents-reports/documentdetail/875761468136575589/beyond-connections-energy-access-redefined). Improvements beyond an already established average level of consumption produce more modest and diffuse gains.

This has direct implications for electrification policy: expanding access to people who do not yet have it produces much greater returns than upgrading infrastructure in areas that are already electrified, at least in terms of basic human well-being.

The upper end of the distribution adds another perspective. Every country that has reached an HDI of 0.9 or above has per-capita electricity consumption of at least 4,000 kWh per year, although not every country above that consumption level has reached the same HDI. At that level, electricity functions as a necessary but not sufficient condition: below the threshold, high human development has not occurred in any documented case; above it, institutional, educational and distributional factors determine whether the available energy potential translates into broadly shared well-being.

## 3. What happens when supply fails

Electricity outages have a documented cost that goes far beyond inconvenience. In countries with unreliable electricity infrastructure, firms invest in backup diesel generators, adding a permanent operating cost that firms in countries with stable supply do not face. World Bank estimates place the cost of interruptions at the equivalent of 5–6% of annual revenue for large firms, a figure that can exceed 20% for small and medium-sized firms with less ability to install their own backup generation [World Bank, Enterprise Surveys](https://www.enterprisesurveys.org/). That differential acts as an implicit tax on economic activity: it appears in no aggregate indicator, but it appears directly in the margins of every firm that bears it.

In healthcare, the consequences can be measured directly in clinical outcomes. Studies in countries with high outage rates show correlations between outage frequency and perinatal mortality, vaccine losses caused by breaks in the cold chain, and cancellation of scheduled surgical procedures.

For households, prolonged outages in summer or winter have a direct effect on morbidity and mortality among vulnerable populations: older adults, patients with chronic conditions who depend on electrical equipment, and families unable to pay for backup alternatives.

## 4. Electricity supply as enabling infrastructure

One way to understand electricity is to treat it not as a final service but as enabling infrastructure: the condition that makes other services with a direct impact on well-being possible.

In those terms, electricity works like drinking water or transport networks: its value is not in itself but in what it unlocks. A drinking-water system has no value if the distribution network loses 40% of the water. A transport system has no value if roads are cut off for half the year. An electricity system has no value if outages are so frequent that critical services cannot operate on it.

{{ include_html("snippets/ia-pib-bienestar-energia/01-electricidad-infraestructura.html") }}

> What matters is not only how much electricity is available, but how reliably it arrives and at what cost to end users. Supply quality is as decisive as quantity.

The next chapter examines how AI enters this ecosystem: what computational demand it creates, what pressure it adds to energy infrastructure, and why increasing hardware efficiency does not necessarily imply declining energy demand. The connection between this chapter and the next is not only technical: if AI requires stable and abundant electricity to operate, and access to that electricity remains unequal globally, the well-being gap that electrification helped reduce could reappear in a new form with AI as the vector.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much electricity is needed for electrification to have a real impact on well-being?**
Studies suggest that the threshold at which effects on health and the economy become consistent is around 500–1,000 kWh per capita per year. Above that level, the system can support food and medicine refrigeration, night-time lighting and small machinery. Below it, access is insufficient for the activities that produce the most visible gains in well-being. The World Bank's Multi-Tier Framework (MTF) formalizes this through five tiers, where the lowest tier—hours of low-intensity lighting—is still far from enabling the productive uses that change a local economy.

**What is the difference between having access to the electricity grid and having effective access to energy?**
The official electrification rate measures whether a connection is available, not whether supply is sufficient for the uses that matter. Many communities with a formal connection receive between two and eight hours of electricity per day or experience voltage drops that make productive equipment unusable. Those households appear in statistics as "having access" even when supply does not reach the MTF thresholds that enable health or economic uses. The relevant data are not the connection itself but the available power, reliability and cost.

**Why is the cost of electricity-supply interruptions so high for firms in developing countries?**
Because where the grid is unreliable, every electricity-dependent firm has to invest in backup generation, usually diesel generators. That cost duplicates part of the investment required for electricity, reduces the competitiveness of local firms relative to firms operating on a stable grid, and acts as a barrier to activities requiring precision equipment or continuous refrigeration. World Bank Enterprise Surveys place the cost of interruptions at between 3% and 15% of annual revenue depending on the sector.

**Is electricity sufficient to produce development, or are other conditions required?**
Electricity is a necessary but not sufficient condition. The most rigorous studies find positive causal effects, but also document that those effects are larger when electrification is combined with complementary investments: transport infrastructure to bring products to market, healthcare systems able to use equipment powered by the grid, or technical training to operate the machinery electrification makes possible. Without those complements, electrification improves indicators but does not produce the same leap suggested by data from countries that already have them.

**How does electricity access connect to the inequality risk created by AI?**
If AI requires computing infrastructure that depends on stable and abundant electricity, countries and regions that already have energy deficits will be excluded from tools that are available in economies with robust grids. The effect is not only access to AI services but also the capacity to adopt the productive processes AI will enable, from assisted medical diagnosis to precision agriculture. The electrification gap can become an AI gap if the first problem is not solved.
